# Response to review

**Revised paper:** "Learning Pursuit Control for Missile Launch Positioning with PPO in a Kinematic 3D Simulator" (`paper/main.pdf`).

**Code:** https://github.com/Akshith-cdr/air-combat-rl-simulation, `pursuit/` package. The policies were trained at commit `b5c8a49` and evaluated at commit `85731ef`.

The whole pipeline was rebuilt so that each point could be fixed at its source, not only in the text. Every number, table and figure in the paper is generated by `paper/make_figures.py` from the saved run outputs.

## M1 — The training and evaluation launch gates did not match
**What the old draft did wrong:** the reviewed draft's flight stages were trained before the 500 m minimum existed. During training the gate was really 0–100 m with cos ATA > 0.95. The text mixed that gate with the later 500 m minimum.

**What changed:** training and evaluation now read one gate from `pursuit/config.py`: 500 m ≤ d ≤ 1000 m and cos ATA > 0.95. A launch is requested on every step, so the missile leaves on the first aligned step inside the band. The text states this once (Sec. III, Eq. 2), and there is no separate training gate any more.

## M2 — The code changed after training
**Fix:** the policies were retrained from committed code.
- **Clean-tree check:** `pursuit/provenance.py` refuses to train or evaluate when there are uncommitted changes.
- **Saved with every result:** every `run.json` and `summary.json` stores the commit hash, the full task configuration and PPO hyperparameters, the package versions and the SHA-256 of the checkpoint files.
- **Reward equation:** Eq. (3) is a transcription of `reward_terms()` in `pursuit/env.py`.
- **Reproducibility check:** re-running all 14 evaluations at a later commit gave identical outcomes in every episode.

## M3 — No baseline
**Added:** pure pursuit and lead pursuit (`pursuit/baselines.py`). They use the same four controls, launch rule, gate, missile and episodes as PPO. Results (Table II, Fig. 3):

| Controller | Six geometries | All-round sweep |
|---|---|---|
| PPO, 5 seeds | 98.98% ± 0.42 | 98.49% ± 0.31 |
| Lead pursuit | 98.7% | 97.5% |
| Pure pursuit | 97.8% | 96.9% |

The baselines are close to PPO, and the paper says so. On the sweep, every PPO seed beats both baselines, but only by 0.6–1.9 points. On the six geometries the controllers overlap. The abstract, discussion and conclusion state that this task alone does not show a need for RL.

## M4 — The test cases were too easy and too narrow
**Added:** an all-round test of 1,008 episodes:
- target at 12 bearings, every 30°;
- 4 target headings;
- 3 ranges (1.5, 3 and 4.5 km);
- 7 seeds per combination.

Hit rates are reported per bearing sector (Fig. 3b). Table III now shows each geometry's bearing and the target's starting heading, with results per controller. No sector is hard for PPO; its seed range stays above about 96% in every sector.

## M5 — Only one training seed
Seeds 1–5 were trained with identical settings. The paper reports mean, standard deviation and range, and shows learning curves of hit rate against training steps for every seed (Fig. 2).

## M6 — The final stage showed no gain; the run history was hard to reproduce
Each seed is now **one from-scratch run**: random initial weights, the final task, no curriculum, no earlier stages, 1.5M steps.
- **Visible gain:** the learning curve goes from 0–2% to 100%. The mean passes 90% at 295k steps, and all seeds reach 100% by 0.69M steps.
- **Remaining weakness, stated in the paper:** the 48-episode development set saturates after 0.69M steps, so it cannot measure any later gain. The two 1,000-episode test suites measure the final policies.

## M7 — The title and framing claimed too much
- **Title:** the suggested title, adopted as given.
- **What is learned:** the paper states throughout that only pursuit flight is learned, and that firing, gating and guidance are fixed rules against a non-reactive target.
- **"Dependable":** removed. The conclusion now calls the controller "competitive with, and slightly better than, simple pursuit guidance in this setting."

## Paper comments
- **Section title:** "Proposed Methodology" is now "Method".
- **Why a kinematic model is enough:** a new paragraph in Sec. IV. The question is geometric (turn rate, speed and lag), and both controllers share the same model. JSBSim is named as the next step.
- **"874 launches at 590–600 m":** no longer presented as skill. Sec. VI says that launches near the band's outer edge are a property of the rule, and that pure pursuit does the same (median launch range 996 m against 993 m for PPO).
- **Collisions:**
  - Any pass within 10 m of the target now ends the episode as a failure. The check uses the swept path within each step. Going below z = 0 also counts as a failure.
  - PPO's 10 collisions all happen *after* launch.
  - Lead pursuit's 9 collisions all happen *before* launch.
- **Trajectories and the cause of each miss:**
  - The full time series of every failed episode is saved, and all 218 failures are classified (Table IV).
  - 198 are missile overshoots. The missile passes 60 m from the target (median), just outside the 50 m fuse, 2.8 s after launch, and cannot turn back.
  - The misses concentrate in launches against crossing targets, at 45–135° aspect (Fig. 4). A typical case is shown in Fig. 5.
- **"Naturally passes through the band":** tested with the baselines. It is true, but it is not specific to learning: pure pursuit does the same.
- **Presentation:**
  - File paths are moved to an appendix, with the public repository link.
  - The text uses "we" throughout.
  - Axis-start notes are kept on the figures whose axes don't start at zero.

## Other corrections found during the revision
- **Target roll at reset:** the earlier simulator started the target with roll = 180°, which clamps to a 90° bank.
- **Target nose direction:** the target's nose pointed in the mirrored direction from its flight path.

Both are fixed (Sec. V), and all results above use the corrected simulator.
